[03/2025 - Current] Typheonix - A Compound-Turbocharger Based Afterburning Turbojet Engine

Typheonix is a small scale gas turbine engine that I am developing to study the physical dynamics that govern gas turbines. I started designing and building this project because I have had a strong desire to build a gas turbine engine for several years and I did not have the resources to undertake such a project until I started planning and designing Typheonix in March of 2025. The Typheonix engine was designed to utilize two turbochargers from an automotive racing application in a twin-spool, compound (or series) configuration. The reason this architecture was chosen for the design is because turbochargers are optimized to run most efficiently in a specific operating regime where variables like mass flow rate, pressure ratio, and intake air temperaature line up. Running two turbochargers in series allows you to feed the compressed air from the outlet of the first turbo's compressor (the low-pressure compressor) into the inlet to the second turbo's compressor (the high-pressure compressor) where the ratio the air is compressed at is compounded or multiplicative. This means that in a compound turbo arrangement the compression ratio or pressure ratio possible with the system is much higher than what is possible with a single turbo system. Additionally, a compound turbo configuration with a low pressure turbo that is identical to the turbo in a single turbo system when compared to the single turbo system, can run at a given mass flow and pressure ratio operating point significantly more thermodynamically efficent than the single turbo. This is because the operating points of the low and high pressure compressors and turbines in the compound turbo system can run at a more efficient operating condition for those specific components, whereas the single turbo system may be outside the most efficient operating windows for it's components. as the single turbo The main purpose I intend for Typheonix is to gain experience and knowledge throught the planning, designing, building and testing phases of the project. The Typheonix engine has been designed to maximize the efficiency of the system in a small window of operational conitions. The design was chosen to maximize the level of static pressure after the low pressure turbine. The reason the design optimizes the static pressure available after the turbine to be as high as possible is to maximize the pressure drop that can be achieved at the nozzle which in turn creates a higher velocity in the gases exiting the nozzle. Initial thermodynamic and flow calculations proved that with the two turbochargers used in my design, and including reasonable estimates for losses throughout the system that the system should be able to create enough static pressure downstream of the low pressure turbine and upstream of the exhaust nozzle to produce a choked flow condition in the nozzle where the exhaust gases in the throat (the region of the nozzle with the smallest cross section area) to reach their own speed of sound and transition into a supersonic flow regime.

High-Level Systems Design & Schematic Diagrams

To gain a better understanding of the high-level overall design architecture and how these design choices would be implemenented in the construction phases of the project, I used the classic approach of writing out schematic diagrams on pencil and paper.

This is the schematic diagram of the "general plumbing" which details the air and gas paths through the ducts connecting the turbochargers and the combustion chamber (or combustor).

This is the schematic diagram of the oil lubrication systems for the engine. The engine has two independent oil lubrication systems, one for each turbocharger. Because each oil system was designed to be tunable to the correct oil pressure and flow rate, both systems are identical, even though the low-pressure and the high-pressure turbo are different. The low and high pressure turbos only differ in oil lubrication requirements in flow rate - the larger, low-pressure turbo needing more oil flow than the smaller high-pressure turbo. Both the low-pressure and high- pressure turbo require identical oil pressure supply. Therefore, I chose to simplify the design and contruction of the oil systems by using identical systems for each turbocharger, that have the ability to be tuned to the requirements of each turbo. Each oil circuit system includes a 3L reservoir, a positive displacement oil pump powered by a 150W motor, a bypass circuit with a needle valve control that bypasses oil after the oil pump back into the oil reservoir, an oil filter, an oil cooler with two draw-through electric cooling fans per oil cooler unit, and finally, a pressure/ temperature junction to monitor the operational parameters of each oil circuit.
